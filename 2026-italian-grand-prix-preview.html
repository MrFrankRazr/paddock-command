2026 Italian Grand Prix Preview: Antonelli Faces a Monza Recovery Race

The championship leader heads to his home Grand Prix with a 59-point advantage but a back-of-grid start, while Norris brings consecutive victories and Ferrari arrives with a Monza-focused upgrade package.

Paddock Command editorial · Published Aug 28, 2026 · Updated Sep 3, 2026
Updated Sep 3 for Monza energy-management intelligence, Thursday driver updates and FIA Heat Hazard
CHAMPIONSHIP LEADERAntonelli · 242 pts
ROUND13 of 23
CIRCUITMonza · 5.793 km
RACE DISTANCE306.72 km

The championship leader starts on the defensive

Kimi Antonelli arrives at Monza with 242 points, 59 clear of George Russell and Lewis Hamilton on 183. Lando Norris is fourth on 159, with Charles Leclerc on 155. The margin remains substantial, but Antonelli’s task has changed: Mercedes has committed to a full power-unit change that will put him at the back of the grid.

Antonelli’s replacement power unit should not be confused with Mercedes’ own ADUO performance upgrade. Mercedes has ruled out introducing that specification at Monza and currently plans to deploy its upgraded power unit in October. The Italian Grand Prix penalty is therefore an allocation decision rather than the debut of a faster Mercedes engine specification.

Antonelli now has a recovery target — and a qualifying role

Antonelli is no longer treating the Monza penalty simply as damage limitation. Speaking Thursday, the championship leader said a top-five finish is a realistic target if Mercedes has sufficient race pace, while acknowledging that Ferrari, McLaren and Red Bull could make progress considerably harder if the leading cars remain closely matched.

His compromised starting position could also change Mercedes’ qualifying tactics. Antonelli confirmed that the team has already discussed ways he could assist George Russell, including potentially providing a tow. He said he will support his team-mate if Mercedes asks him to do so.

That creates an unusual Saturday dynamic. Antonelli can prioritise Sunday preparation while Mercedes potentially turns his otherwise compromised qualifying session into an advantage for Russell — one of the two drivers currently 59 points behind him in the championship.

That turns Round 13 from another exercise in championship management into one of the most important recovery drives of Antonelli’s season. Heavy points would limit the damage. A victory would require an exceptional race.

Paddock Command watch pointDo not judge Antonelli’s weekend by qualifying position alone. Underlying qualifying pace, Mercedes straight-line performance and the size of the gap to the leading cars will say more about his recovery potential.

Norris brings the strongest recent momentum

Norris has won the last two Grands Prix, Hungary and the Netherlands. The 83-point gap to Antonelli remains large, but consecutive victories have changed the competitive trajectory and Monza gives McLaren another opportunity while the championship leader begins with a track-position disadvantage.

Monza asks different questions from Zandvoort. Low drag, straight-line efficiency, braking confidence and energy deployment become central. Friday practice will show whether McLaren’s recent form transfers to this environment.

Russell and Hamilton have an opening

Russell and Hamilton enter the weekend tied on 183 points. With Antonelli starting from the rear, both have a rare opportunity to take a meaningful amount out of the championship lead.

Russell can become Mercedes’ primary front-running threat while Antonelli works through traffic. Hamilton arrives at Ferrari’s home race with the team bringing fresh development for Monza.

Ferrari brings more than home-race emotion

Ferrari has now confirmed that it will introduce its second power-unit upgrade under Formula 1’s Additional Development and Upgrade Opportunities system at its home Grand Prix. The team introduced its first ADUO-influenced specification in Austria and was granted two upgrade opportunities after its power unit was assessed as more than four percent behind the championship benchmark. The Monza package therefore represents Ferrari’s second ADUO development step and also includes aerodynamic revisions and track-specific components aimed at efficiency on a circuit dominated by long full-throttle sections and major braking events.

Ferrari’s second ADUO upgrade is confirmed for Monza

The second ADUO confirmation gives Ferrari’s home-race development program a clearer competitive purpose. Monza is one of the most revealing venues for power-unit performance because of its long full-throttle sections, energy-deployment demands and extreme emphasis on straight-line efficiency.

Hamilton believes Ferrari can fight for the Monza win

Lewis Hamilton has raised the competitive stakes around Ferrari’s home-race upgrade. Speaking at Monza on Thursday, Hamilton said he strongly believes Ferrari has the capability to win, although he stressed that the engine development represents another incremental improvement rather than a single transformative step.

The significance will become clearer on Friday. Ferrari needs the updated power unit and low-drag package to reduce the race-pace deficit Hamilton highlighted after Zandvoort, while Monza’s long full-throttle sections provide an unusually direct test of the latest engine development.

For the Tifosi, the opportunity is substantial: Hamilton enters the weekend tied with George Russell for second in the championship, and Ferrari is trying to convert its home-race development program into its strongest challenge yet to Mercedes.

For Paddock Command, Friday’s useful comparison will be Ferrari’s speed-trap performance, deployment consistency and whether Lewis Hamilton and Charles Leclerc can preserve braking confidence while running a low-drag configuration.

The Scuderia has also unveiled a one-off Michael Schumacher-inspired livery for the SF-26s of Lewis Hamilton and Charles Leclerc. The tribute marks 30 years since Schumacher joined Ferrari and incorporates details including his signature, seven stars representing his seven world championships and retro-style driver numbers.

The tribute will command attention from the Tifosi, but the competitive question is more important for Paddock Command: does Ferrari merely look different at Monza, or does the SF-26 actually move closer to the front? Friday practice will provide the first useful evidence.

Hamilton sits level with Russell in the championship while Leclerc is only four points behind Norris. A competitive Ferrari would add another layer to a weekend that already looks more open than the standings alone suggest.

Leclerc confirms Ferrari expects additional power

Charles Leclerc has confirmed that Ferrari expects its updated Monza power unit to provide additional power, while cautioning that the size of the improvement will only become clear on track.

The Ferrari driver also acknowledged that straight-line performance remains a relative weakness against the team’s main competitors. Monza therefore becomes an unusually clean evaluation of the second ADUO development step: Ferrari must not only produce more power, but deploy it effectively across some of the longest full-throttle sections of the season.

McLaren brings a Monza-specific technical answer

McLaren’s recent resurgence is no longer only a form story. The team will introduce its rotating rear-wing concept — internally called the H-Wing — at Monza, giving the MCL40 a new low-drag tool specifically suited to Formula 1’s fastest traditional circuit.

The concept rotates 180 degrees between cornering and straight-line modes. McLaren first brought a prototype to Austria before running a revised version during Friday practice in Hungary. Monza is the first race weekend where the team has committed to using the H-Wing on both cars.

The timing matters. McLaren has already introduced a substantial sequence of aerodynamic updates across Hungary and Zandvoort, including floor work, diffuser changes, a new rear wing, revised engine-cover bodywork and front-wheel changes. Lando Norris then converted that progress into consecutive Grand Prix victories, both from pole position.

Monza will therefore test more than whether Norris’s recent form travels to a different circuit type. It will test whether McLaren’s development trajectory has produced a car capable of challenging Mercedes and Ferrari in an extreme low-drag configuration.

Paddock Command watch pointIf McLaren retains its recent cornering performance while gaining straight-line efficiency from the H-Wing, Norris becomes a considerably more serious Monza threat than his championship position alone suggests.

Monza’s biggest technical question may be energy, not drag

The 2026 Italian Grand Prix will test more than conventional low-drag efficiency. Formula 1’s new power-unit rules make electrical deployment a defining variable on circuits with long full-throttle sections, and Monza is the most extreme version of that challenge so far.

The FIA’s event-specific power-unit parameters underline how session-dependent the problem will be. The maximum recharge allowance is 7.5 MJ per lap in free practice, 5.0 MJ in qualifying, 7.0 MJ in the race when Overtake is not active and 7.5 MJ when Overtake is active. Teams must therefore optimize deployment differently across Friday, Saturday and Sunday rather than work around one universal Monza energy limit.

Pirelli’s official weekend analysis also expects the long straights to create an additional energy-management challenge with the 2026 power units. Team simulations point to lap times roughly two seconds faster than last year even though some braking zones are expected to be approached at reduced speeds.

Friday’s useful comparison therefore goes beyond the speed trap. Paddock Command will be watching where each manufacturer begins losing electrical deployment, how much speed is retained near the end of the straights and whether Ferrari’s upgraded power unit can reduce one of the SF-26’s acknowledged weaknesses.

Why Antonelli can still recover

A back-of-grid start at Monza is costly, but the circuit offers more overtaking opportunity than many venues. Slipstream effects, long straights and heavy braking zones can help a fast car move forward.

The recovery still requires discipline. Antonelli must avoid first-lap trouble, clear slower traffic efficiently and protect the tyres. Mercedes must also decide when clean air is worth an early stop and when strategic flexibility is more valuable.

Red Bull’s Monza driver line-up is settled

Isack Hadjar will miss the Italian Grand Prix as his recovery from a wrist injury continues. Liam Lawson will therefore remain alongside Max Verstappen at Red Bull for a second consecutive weekend, while Yuki Tsunoda again fills Lawson’s Racing Bulls seat alongside Arvid Lindblad.

Lawson finished seventh at Zandvoort after qualifying eighth, giving Red Bull useful evidence that he can step into the RB22 with limited preparation. Tsunoda finished 11th in his return with Racing Bulls.

The continuity removes one uncertainty heading into Friday practice. Both Red Bull-owned teams can begin Monza with the same temporary line-ups used at Zandvoort rather than introducing another driver change.

Red Bull will also surrender Verstappen’s FP1 running

Ayumu Iwasa is scheduled to drive Max Verstappen’s Red Bull during the opening practice session. That means Red Bull will start its Monza programme without Verstappen’s direct feedback while Liam Lawson continues to substitute for Isack Hadjar.

The significance is primarily technical rather than competitive. Verstappen returns for FP2, but Red Bull will have less time than usual to validate its low-drag setup and tyre behaviour with its lead driver before qualifying preparation begins.

Tyres make Friday practice more important

Pirelli has selected the C3, C4 and C5 compounds for Monza, giving teams the softest tyre combination in the 2026 range. Each driver receives two sets of the hard C3, three sets of the medium C4 and eight sets of the soft C5, with an extra set of softs reserved for drivers who reach Q3.

FIA activates Monza Heat Hazard protocol

The FIA has formally declared a Heat Hazard for the Italian Grand Prix after its official weather service forecast a race-period heat index above the 31°C regulatory threshold. Teams must therefore fit Formula 1’s Driver Cooling System throughout the weekend, while drivers can choose whether to use the cooling vest itself.

Official forecasts point to temperatures in the mid-30s Celsius across the weekend. Motorsport.com reports approximately 36°C during Friday FP2, 35°C around Saturday qualifying and 33°C at the Sunday 15:00 race start. Formula1.com also forecasts track temperatures as high as roughly 56°C on Friday and 57°C on Saturday and Sunday.

That elevates tyre degradation, rear-tyre temperature control and cooling efficiency from background concerns to core Friday intelligence. FP2 should be the most representative opportunity to assess how the C5 soft behaves in conditions closest to the race weekend’s thermal peak.

Heat puts the C5 under an immediate spotlight

Friday is forecast to be hot and dry, with ambient temperatures reaching about 34°C and track temperatures potentially climbing to roughly 56°C. Saturday is expected to be similarly hot, with track temperatures reaching around 57°C, while Sunday is forecast to remain warm and predominantly dry.

That makes the C5 more than a qualifying-pace story. Teams will need to understand how quickly the soft tyre overheats, how much performance it retains across multiple laps and whether Monza’s low-drag configurations make rear-tyre management more difficult under acceleration.

For Paddock Command, Friday’s most useful comparison will be whether the fastest cars can produce competitive one-lap pace without compromising degradation over longer runs.

That matters because Monza already forces an extreme aerodynamic compromise. Cars need minimal drag on the long straights without surrendering too much stability under braking or traction through the slower corners. The softer tyre range adds another setup variable before qualifying and the race.

For Paddock Command, the useful Friday signal is not simply the fastest FP1 lap. Watch long-run degradation, braking consistency and whether teams can retain tyre performance while running their lowest-drag configurations. McLaren’s H-Wing makes that comparison especially relevant: if the MCL40 combines its new rear-wing solution with stable tyre behaviour, Norris’s recent form may transfer convincingly to Monza.

Alpine will also begin the weekend with reserve driver Paul Aron replacing Pierre Gasly in FP1. Aron will then return to Enstone for Friday-night simulator work as Alpine evaluates its latest upgrade package and fine-tunes the setup for Saturday.

Paddock Command watch pointThe C5 soft is now one of Friday’s key references. Teams that understand its operating window quickly can spend more of FP2 refining race balance instead of chasing one-lap behaviour.

Five questions that will define Monza

1. How far can Antonelli recover?

The finishing position matters, but so does the points loss relative to Russell, Hamilton and Norris.

2. Can Norris make it three consecutive victories?

McLaren has momentum. Monza will show whether its recent performance represents a broader competitive step.

3. Can Ferrari’s upgrade deliver immediately?

The home race provides a particularly clear test of Ferrari’s latest power-unit and efficiency development.

4. How quickly do teams understand the C5 soft?

The softest tyre in Pirelli’s Monza allocation should be central to qualifying preparation. Teams that establish its operating window early can spend FP2 refining race balance rather than chasing one-lap behaviour.

5. Does the championship gap finally contract?

Antonelli has spent much of 2026 building enough margin to absorb difficult weekends. Monza is exactly the type of weekend that margin was built for.

Weekend schedule

Practice 1 and Practice 2 run Friday, September 4. Practice 3 and qualifying follow Saturday, September 5. The Italian Grand Prix starts at 15:00 local time on Sunday, September 6 and runs for 53 laps over 306.72 kilometres.
