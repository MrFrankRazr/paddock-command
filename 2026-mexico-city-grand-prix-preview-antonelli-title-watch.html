Saturday driver change: Hirakawa replaces Ocon at Haas

Haas confirmed on Saturday, October 31, that reserve driver Ryo Hirakawa will take Esteban Ocon’s seat for the remainder of the Mexico City Grand Prix weekend. The 32-year-old Japanese driver is scheduled to run in FP3 and qualifying before making his Formula 1 race debut in Sunday’s 71-lap Grand Prix, alongside Oliver Bearman. This is a change to the race-driver line-up, not merely one of Friday’s mandatory rookie practice substitutions.

Hirakawa already completed FP1 for Haas on Friday, finishing 15th in his third free-practice outing of 2026. He returned the car to Ocon for FP2, when Ocon finished 14th. Hirakawa is a two-time World Endurance Championship winner and a 2022 Le Mans 24 Hours winner. Haas confirmed the replacement but gave no explanation for Ocon’s absence and said it would make no further statement at that time. No medical, disciplinary or contractual reason should be inferred from the announcement.

The immediate sporting consequence is a changed Haas driver pairing for qualifying and the race. Hirakawa now has one final practice session to prepare for a full Grand Prix weekend in Mexico City’s thin air, where braking, cooling and tyre preparation are unusually demanding. The final starting position will depend on qualifying and any applicable FIA grid decisions; no Saturday classification was available at the time of this update. Friday practice: Leclerc sets the benchmark twice

Ferrari’s Charles Leclerc was quickest in both Mexico City practice sessions on Friday, October 30. He opened with a 1:18.128 in FP1, 0.659 seconds ahead of Kimi Antonelli, and improved to 1:17.827 in FP2. His second-session margin was much tighter: Lando Norris was only 0.103 seconds behind, George Russell 0.106 seconds behind, Antonelli 0.217 seconds behind and Max Verstappen 0.311 seconds behind.

The headline is Ferrari’s one-lap speed, not a settled qualifying hierarchy. Norris and Russell were separated by just 0.003 seconds, with the top five covered by 0.311 seconds. Fuel loads, tyre condition, traffic and programme differences are not publicly standardised; Friday rankings should be treated as an early performance indicator rather than a forecast of Saturday’s grid.

FP1 was unusually difficult to compare because ten teams used practice-only driver substitutions. Frederik Vesti finished third for Mercedes, Lewis Hamilton fourth for Ferrari and Ayumu Iwasa fifth for Red Bull. With most regular drivers returning in FP2, the second session is the more useful Friday comparison, although it is still not a like-for-like test.

Antonelli returns and keeps the title equation intact

Antonelli completed both Friday sessions after missing Thursday media commitments while recovering from a viral illness. He finished second in FP1 and fourth in FP2. That confirms he returned to on-track running; it does not establish his medical condition beyond what Mercedes previously disclosed. His championship position is unchanged by practice: 365 points to Russell’s 242 and Hamilton’s 241.

Ninth place or better on Sunday still guarantees Antonelli the title even if Russell wins. He has nine Grand Prix victories this season, so a 100-point advantage after Mexico would be sufficient on countback with four races left. Russell’s FP2 third place, only 0.106 seconds off Leclerc, is a reminder that Mercedes has two cars in the fight for Saturday’s front rows.

Friday stewards decisions: Verstappen, Lawson, Audi and Bottas

Leclerc’s pace came with a reliability warning for Ferrari. Hamilton stopped on track during FP2 after reporting a loss of power and was classified 11th. The cause and any knock-on consequences for qualifying have not been established in the official practice report. Ferrari must establish the problem before Saturday rather than assume that Leclerc’s lap-time advantage applies to both cars.

Max Verstappen and Liam Lawson made contact in FP2 after running side by side between Turns 12 and 14, scattering debris and causing a brief red flag. Following stewards hearings, both drivers received one-place grid penalties for Sunday’s race and one penalty point apiece. Formula 1 confirmed the decisions at 04:58 UTC on October 31, citing the stewards’ findings that both manoeuvres were unnecessary and that teams had previously been warned about practice-session conduct. The final grid will be confirmed after qualifying and application of penalties. Audi’s Gabriel Bortoleto was delayed by a technical issue and appeared late in FP2.

Cadillac’s Valtteri Bottas returned for FP2 after Colton Herta completed the planned FP1 substitution. Formula 1 also reported five new power-unit elements beyond Bottas’s seasonal allocation, amounting to a cumulative 50-place penalty. Under the applicable regulation, a penalty exceeding 15 places places a qualifying-classified driver at the back of the grid. The final starting position remains subject to qualifying and the official grid.

The championship equation: why ninth place is enough

After winning the United States Grand Prix on October 25, Antonelli has 365 points, Russell 242 and Lewis Hamilton 241. The Mercedes driver leads Russell by 123 and Hamilton by 124 with five Grands Prix remaining, including Mexico City. Four races will remain after Sunday, with a maximum of 100 Grand Prix points available to any driver.

A ninth-place finish scores two points. Even if Russell wins Mexico City and collects 25, Antonelli would leave with a 100-point advantage. That is sufficient on countback: Antonelli already has nine victories in 2026, while Russell has three; even winning all five remaining races would leave Russell with only eight wins. The same P9 result also covers Hamilton and every other challenger.

Tenth place would also be enough unless Russell wins. Antonelli can even clinch without scoring if neither Russell nor Hamilton wins the race. Those are mathematical possibilities, not a forecast of the finishing order. No championship trophy has yet been secured.

Driver availability: both Antonelli and Bottas returned Friday

Mercedes’ October 29 announcement excused Antonelli from Thursday media duties while he recovered from a viral illness; he then ran in both Friday practice sessions, finishing P2 and P4. No change to his race entry has been announced. The first on-track evidence is encouraging for participation, but Friday lap times cannot quantify the effects of illness.

Cadillac’s Bottas also missed Thursday media commitments and then took part in FP2, as planned, after Herta drove his car in FP1. The more significant new sporting development is Bottas’s cumulative power-unit grid penalty, detailed above. Pérez also drove in both Friday sessions, finishing 18th in FP2.

Why Mexico City is a different technical test

The 4.304-kilometre Autódromo Hermanos Rodríguez sits more than 2,000 metres above sea level and stages a 71-lap Grand Prix. Thin air reduces aerodynamic load and makes cooling management a bigger concern, even when teams use high-downforce wing settings. A car that worked well through Austin’s flowing corners may not carry the same advantage into Mexico.

The long run to Turn 1 also creates a distinctive opening-lap problem. Formula 1 places the first major braking point roughly 830 metres beyond pole position, giving the cars behind an opportunity to use the slipstream before the field compresses into the opening complex. Later in the lap, the stadium section rewards precision at relatively low speed.

The 2025 race offers a useful reference, not a prediction: Lando Norris won from pole ahead of Charles Leclerc and Max Verstappen. Verstappen has five Mexico wins, the most of any driver. Current form and 2026 car characteristics will matter more than past results alone.

Tyres: Pirelli goes softer than Austin

Pirelli has nominated C3 as Hard, C4 as Medium and C5 as Soft for Mexico City. That is the softest trio in its 2026 range and a step softer at the hard end than last year’s Mexico selection. Austin used C2/C3/C4, so direct conclusions from the Texas one-stop strategies would be premature.

The strategic questions are whether the C3 can deliver a long, competitive stint; how quickly the C5 degrades in race conditions; and whether the warm-up advantage of the softer tyres is worth an extra stop. Friday supplied initial track evidence, but comparable long-run data and Saturday’s track evolution remain essential. No race-winning strategy can be established from the published Friday classifications alone.

Driver form: Friday pace tightens the front-running picture

Antonelli’s Austin pole-to-win performance was his ninth victory of the season, achieved with a controlled one-stop medium-to-hard strategy. Russell finished eighth in Texas, leaving the title gap at 123 points. Antonelli has the strongest championship position; Russell’s remaining opportunity depends on an extraordinary swing.

Ferrari enters with Charles Leclerc on consecutive second-place finishes and Lewis Hamilton fourth in Austin. Verstappen took third in Texas, extending the six-race podium streak tracked by Paddock Command, and brings five previous Mexico victories. Norris finished fifth in Austin after tyre difficulties; McLaren needs stronger race execution if it is to resist Red Bull in the Constructors’ Championship.

Friday’s FP2 added a fresh layer to that form picture: Leclerc P1, Norris P2, Russell P3, Antonelli P4 and Verstappen P5, all within 0.311 seconds. The team contest is less settled than the Drivers’ Championship. Mercedes leads with 607 points, Ferrari has 474, and Red Bull and McLaren are tied on 355. That tie makes every point in Mexico consequential even if Antonelli seals the individual title.

Pérez signs Cadillac extension through at least 2029 before milestone home race

Cadillac confirmed on Wednesday, October 28, that Sergio Pérez has signed a multi-year extension keeping him with its Formula 1 team until at least 2029. The agreement adds long-term certainty to the American entrant’s first season and arrives just before Pérez is due to make his 300th Formula 1 start at his home Grand Prix. The six-time Grand Prix winner returned to the grid with Cadillac in 2026 after a year away from racing.

The commitment is significant because Cadillac is still building its competitive foundations. Neither Pérez nor teammate Valtteri Bottas has scored a championship point for the new team this season, and the 2027 second race seat has not been definitively settled. Pérez’s extension confirms his long-term future; it does not by itself establish Bottas’s 2027 status or Colton Herta’s eligibility for a race seat.

Cadillac leadership has framed Pérez’s experience and technical feedback as a source of continuity while the team develops. In Mexico, the immediate sporting task remains difficult: the altitude raises cooling demands, tyre preparation matters, and a packed home crowd cannot manufacture lap time. The Mexico City organisers scheduled a Pérez/Cadillac public show run on Paseo de la Reforma for October 28 as part of the 300-start celebrations; this article does not assume an unverified demonstration result.

Weekend weather: Saturday and Sunday outlook

Formula 1’s October 28 pre-weekend forecast gave Friday a 30% rain chance; that is now a historical forecast, not a live observation. The same forecast projected around 27°C with a 20% rain chance for Saturday and dry conditions around 27°C on Sunday. These are older projections and should be checked again before qualifying and the race.

Saturday’s main variables remain track temperature, tyre preparation and traffic. Friday’s practice order shows how small the front-running gaps are; tyre degradation and longer-run competitiveness still require more representative comparisons.

Friday FP1: ten temporary substitutions completed

Ten teams fielded young or reserve drivers in Friday’s opening practice, leaving Ferrari as the only team to run its regular pairing throughout FP1. These began as one-session assignments; Haas subsequently confirmed that Hirakawa would also replace Ocon for the rest of the weekend. The Friday substitutes were Frederik Vesti (Mercedes, for Russell), Ayumu Iwasa (Red Bull, for Verstappen), Leonardo Fornaroli (McLaren, for Norris), Nikola Tsolov (Racing Bulls, for Lawson), Ryō Hirakawa (Haas, for Ocon), Colton Herta (Cadillac, for Bottas), Gabriele Minì (Alpine, for Colapinto), Luke Browning (Williams, for Albon), Freddie Slater (Audi, for Bortoleto) and Jak Crawford (Aston Martin, for Stroll).

Vesti was third fastest, Iwasa fifth and Tsolov 12th. Slater’s Audi outing was interrupted after the car left the garage with a cooling fan still attached to the right radiator intake. The FIA stewards’ October 30 Document 22 confirms a €7,500 fine for Audi for releasing the car in an unsafe condition; the sanction was against the team, not a grid penalty for Slater. Crawford stopped on track late in FP1, prompting a Virtual Safety Car. Herta completed his Cadillac running and Bottas returned for FP2. The completed session confirms the rookie programme, but Saturday brought two separate developments: Haas promoted Hirakawa into the race seat, and specialist reports questioned Herta’s Super Licence points total.

Herta’s Super Licence status: one point still missing, reports say

Cadillac reserve driver Colton Herta completed Friday FP1 in place of Valtteri Bottas, but specialist reporting published on October 30–31 indicates the American remains on 39 of the 40 points needed for an FIA Formula 1 Super Licence. The Race reported that an error in the displayed 2025 IndyCar standings had placed Herta ahead of Felix Rosenqvist despite losing the tie-break; the corrected seventh-place classification reduced the Super Licence points from that season. Crash.net independently reported the same 39-point total.

Herta’s Mexico appearance was his fifth FP1 outing of the year, but the reports say only four of those outings earned qualifying Super Licence points. A further eligible FP1 appearance could supply the missing point, subject to FIA rules and confirmation. This is a reported eligibility clarification, not an FIA-issued race-entry decision; Cadillac has not announced a change to its Mexico City race drivers. What Saturday must answer

Haas’s last-minute race-driver change adds Hirakawa’s preparation and race debut to Saturday’s watch list. Ferrari has the Friday benchmark, but the FP2 top five were separated by only 0.311 seconds. Saturday’s FP3 and qualifying must show whether Leclerc’s advantage survives track evolution, fuel-load changes and rivals’ final setup work. Norris and Russell were nearly level in FP2, and Antonelli remained within 0.217 seconds of the fastest lap.

Hamilton’s power loss requires a confirmed technical explanation before his Saturday programme can be assessed. Verstappen and Lawson must account for their confirmed one-place grid drops when the official Sunday grid is issued; the final starting positions cannot be calculated before qualifying. Bottas’s qualifying result will determine how the cumulative power-unit penalty is applied on the official grid. Audi’s €7,500 fine has no direct grid effect on Slater, who was an FP1 substitute.

Sunday’s championship target is unchanged: Antonelli needs ninth place or better to guarantee the title. A Friday practice position does not award points, and the weekend remains open until the official race classification.
