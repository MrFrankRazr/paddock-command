CIRCUIT INTELLIGENCE · SEPANG · SEP 28

Sepang Circuit Intelligence: Tyre Strategy, Heat and the Unknowns Facing F1’s Return to Malaysia

The tyre supplier has made its call. Now teams must solve a high-speed, abrasive circuit that has not hosted modern Formula 1 machinery since 2017.

TYRE ALLOCATIONC2 / C3 / C4
CIRCUIT5.543 km
RACE56 laps
TITLE GAP66 points

The first major strategic decision is already confirmed

For Formula 1’s October 2–4 return to Sepang, Pirelli has nominated C2 as Hard, C3 as Medium and C4 as Soft. The intermediate selection differs from the softer C3/C4/C5 allocation used at Baku and planned for Singapore. It is the first firm strategic reference point ahead of Friday practice.

According to Pirelli, Sepang places medium overall stress on tyres, but its asphalt is particularly abrasive and has not been resurfaced since F1 last raced there in 2017. Rather than select the hardest compounds, the supplier aims to narrow the performance difference between one-stop and two-stop approaches and create more race-strategy options. That is an intention, not a guarantee: Friday’s long runs will determine the actual degradation picture.

Why the asphalt matters

Abrasive asphalt can increase tyre wear and influence the trade-off between early pace and stint length. Teams must learn how the 2026 cars interact with a surface for which their most recent Formula 1 race data is nine years old. Friday’s long-run comparisons will be more useful than assumptions based on the 2017 race.

One stop or two? Keep both scenarios open

A one-stop strategy could reward drivers who protect their tyres and limit time spent in the pit lane. Two stops could allow faster stints if tyre degradation or thermal limitations make the extra stop worthwhile. Pirelli’s stated objective is to encourage that strategic choice. Neither route should be presented as the winning strategy before practice data and weather conditions are available.

Heat, humidity and the rain variable

Sepang is renowned for hot, humid conditions and sudden tropical downpours. Teams must balance tyre-temperature control, car cooling and the possibility of rapidly changing grip. A wet session could also reduce the value of dry long-run comparisons. A precise Sunday forecast will be assessed closer to race day rather than treated as settled this early in the week.

The aerodynamic compromise

Sepang’s fast, flowing Turns 5 and 6 demand high-speed balance, while long straights and the final Turn 15 hairpin place a premium on efficient drag levels, braking and traction. Teams coming from low-drag Baku face a different setup problem: finding enough aerodynamic support without giving away too much straight-line speed.

Experience, simulation and an unfamiliar generation of cars

Formula 1 last raced at Sepang in 2017, when Max Verstappen won. Drivers with previous experience can draw on the layout and environmental demands, but the present cars, tyres and operating conditions mean all teams will need to validate their simulator work on track.

Championship Watch: Russell’s next test

George Russell arrives after winning the Azerbaijan Grand Prix and reducing Kimi Antonelli’s championship advantage to 66 points, 302–236. Sepang will test whether Mercedes’ Baku speed translates to a very different circuit. The points gap is unchanged until Sunday’s race; practice performance alone cannot alter the standings.

What to watch in FP1 and FP2

  • Medium- and hard-compound long-run degradation on the abrasive surface.
  • How quickly the C4 Soft reaches and leaves its effective temperature window.
  • High-speed balance through Turns 5–6 against straight-line performance.
  • Cooling choices and tyre-temperature management in Malaysia’s conditions.
  • Whether changing weather disrupts comparable long-run data.

Confirmed weekend schedule

Friday October 2: FP1 12:30 and FP2 16:00. Saturday October 3: FP3 12:30 and qualifying 16:00. Sunday October 4: the 56-lap Grand Prix begins at 15:00. All times are Malaysia local (UTC+8) and remain subject to official timetable changes.
