F1 Moves Toward Turbo V8 Engines for Its Next Power Unit Era

Formula 1 stakeholders have broadly agreed the architecture of a simpler, lighter 3.0-litre turbo V8 power unit with substantially reduced hybrid output, while key details and the introduction date remain under discussion.

Paddock Command editorial · Published Sep 23, 2026
Emerging regulatory plan · Final regulations not yet approved
ENGINE3.0L Turbo V8
ELECTRIC MOTOR~100 kW
WEIGHT TARGET50+ kg lighter
TIMING2030 under consideration

Formula 1's next major technical reset is beginning to take shape — and the V8 is moving closer to a return.

F1 stakeholders have reached broad agreement on the fundamental architecture of the championship's next-generation power unit, with current plans centered on a 3.0-litre turbocharged V8, a significantly smaller hybrid system and a major reduction in power-unit weight.

The regulations are not final. Discussions between the FIA and Formula 1's power-unit manufacturers are continuing, while several important details — including the exact introduction date and future overtaking system — remain unresolved.

But the direction is becoming considerably clearer.

The V8 is coming back — but not the old V8

Formula 1 last raced with V8 engines in 2013 before moving to the turbo-hybrid V6 formula in 2014.

The emerging next-generation concept would restore eight-cylinder engines, but it would not recreate the naturally aspirated power units remembered from the previous era.

Instead, the proposed engine is a 3.0-litre V8 retaining turbocharging.

A naturally aspirated alternative was evaluated during discussions, but one significant obstacle is noise. Modern Formula 1 visits numerous temporary and street circuits operating under local noise restrictions, making an unrestricted naturally aspirated engine difficult to accommodate across the existing calendar.

A smaller turbocharger therefore offers a compromise: Formula 1 can move toward a more traditional racing-engine architecture while retaining greater control over sound levels and performance.

Hybrid power stays — but its role shrinks

Electrification is also expected to survive the next regulations. Its influence, however, could become dramatically smaller.

The proposed V8 architecture is expected to use an electric motor producing approximately 100kW, compared with the roughly 350kW electrical component incorporated into Formula 1's current-generation power units.

That would shift considerably more emphasis back toward the internal-combustion engine while retaining a hybrid element relevant to manufacturers.

A 50kg weight reduction is being targeted

Weight is another major objective. The next power unit is being targeted to weigh at least 50 kilograms less than the current-generation system.

Reducing the size and complexity of the electrical hardware should contribute significantly toward that objective and support Formula 1's wider push toward lighter cars.

Push-to-pass could become part of the formula

The next regulations may also change how drivers access additional performance when attacking another car.

One concept under consideration resembles the push-to-pass system used in IndyCar, giving drivers access to a temporary additional power boost under defined conditions. No final decision has been made.

Why Formula 1 is already looking beyond 2026

The discussion comes during the first season of Formula 1's newest power-unit regulations. The FIA and stakeholders have already agreed changes for 2027 and 2028 intended to rebalance combustion and electrical contribution after energy management became a major feature of the 2026 formula.

The emerging V8 proposal goes considerably further. Rather than continuing to increase electrical complexity, Formula 1 appears prepared to make its next power unit simpler, lighter and more combustion-focused while retaining a smaller hybrid component.

When would the new engines arrive?

2030 is currently under consideration, but the date has not been finalized. Further meetings between the FIA and manufacturers will determine whether that timetable is achievable and settle the remaining technical regulations.

For now, the significance is the direction rather than the date: Formula 1's next engine increasingly looks like a turbocharged V8 with substantially less electrical power, less weight and a simpler technical philosophy.

Paddock Command watch pointThe next decisive milestones are confirmation of the introduction year, final hybrid output, turbo specification and whether the proposed push-to-pass system enters the regulations. Until formal approval, the V8 architecture should be treated as an advanced regulatory plan rather than a completed rulebook.
