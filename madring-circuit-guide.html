CIRCUIT GUIDE · SPANISH GRAND PRIX

Madring Circuit Guide: What F1’s New Madrid Track Will Demand

Formula 1 heads directly from Monza to a completely new 5.416 km, 22-corner Madrid circuit where simulation, tyre understanding and rapid setup learning will matter immediately.

Paddock Command editorial · Updated Sep 9, 2026
Spanish Grand Prix · Sep 11–13, 2026
LENGTH5.416 km
CORNERS22
RACE57 laps
SIGNATURELa Monumental · 24%

A new circuit arrives at a critical point in the season

Formula 1 arrives in Madrid with something teams rarely encounter during a modern championship season: a circuit with no Formula 1 race history.

Madring hosts the Spanish Grand Prix from September 11–13 as Round 14 of the 2026 season, immediately after Kimi Antonelli transformed a 19th-place start at Monza into another championship-extending victory.

That combination makes Madrid more than simply the next stop on the calendar. It is a fresh engineering problem arriving at a point when the championship leader has just strengthened his position.

The numbers establish the challenge

Madring measures 5.416 kilometres and the Spanish Grand Prix will run for 57 laps, producing an official race distance of 308.524 kilometres.

The circuit contains 22 corners and combines public-road sections with purpose-built infrastructure. Unlike established venues, there is no Formula 1 race lap record, no recent Grand Prix tyre history and no meaningful historical race strategy to reuse.

Every team therefore begins with simulation rather than accumulated track evidence.

Spanish Grand Prix weekend schedule

Madring’s first Formula 1 race weekend begins Friday, September 11, with three practice sessions preceding qualifying and Sunday’s 57-lap Grand Prix.

Friday, September 11 FP1 — 13:30 Madrid / 4:30 AM PT / 7:30 AM ET
FP2 — 17:00 Madrid / 8:00 AM PT / 11:00 AM ET

Saturday, September 12 FP3 — 12:30 Madrid / 3:30 AM PT / 6:30 AM ET
Qualifying — 16:00 Madrid / 7:00 AM PT / 10:00 AM ET

Sunday, September 13 Spanish Grand Prix — 15:00 Madrid / 6:00 AM PT / 9:00 AM ET

The traditional non-Sprint format gives teams three practice sessions before qualifying. At an entirely new circuit, that Friday running carries unusual importance: FP1 establishes the first meaningful competitive baseline, while FP2 should begin revealing long-run tyre behaviour and the degree to which teams have converged on workable setups.

La Monumental is the defining corner

The feature likely to define Madring visually and technically is Turn 12, known as La Monumental.

The banked section stretches for roughly half a kilometre and reaches 24% banking, making it one of the most unusual sustained corners on the Formula 1 calendar.

Banking changes the normal relationship between aerodynamic load, tyre loading and corner speed. Drivers can potentially carry significant speed through the turn, but the sustained lateral load will also make tyre temperature and car balance important reference points during practice.

The tyre load is now an especially important part of that equation. Current pre-event technical information indicates La Monumental will generate the highest vertical tyre load of the Formula 1 season. Across an approximately 550-metre banked section, teams will be watching not only lateral loading but tyre temperature, carcass behaviour and whether repeated laps create degradation that simulation has underestimated.

La Monumental’s challenge goes beyond tyre load. Sustained banking places unusually large vertical forces through suspension components, while the prolonged lateral and vertical loading may also test power-unit lubrication. Those mechanical signals should be monitored closely during Friday’s longer runs.

Madrid is not a conventional street circuit

Madring uses both public roads and private land, giving it a hybrid street-and-permanent-circuit character rather than the narrow profile associated with some traditional city races.

Formula 1's current circuit information also points to varied corner types, significant elevation change and straight-line speeds approaching 340 km/h.

The broader competitive question is whether the layout produces natural overtaking opportunities or makes qualifying track position unusually valuable.

The biggest unknown is setup compromise

Teams normally arrive at established circuits with years of aerodynamic, suspension and tyre information. Madrid removes much of that reference.

Cars will need enough downforce to remain stable through the longer loaded corners while still limiting drag through the quicker sections. Ride-height and mechanical-platform control may also matter as teams learn how the real surface compares with simulation.

The first Friday question will therefore not simply be who sets the fastest lap. Paddock Command will be watching which cars achieve useful balance without excessive setup changes between runs.

Interruptions could shape qualifying

Madrid’s lack of Formula 1 race history also increases the operational risk.

Carlos Sainz has warned that evidence from earlier Formula 3 running points toward the possibility of safety cars, red flags and qualifying yellow flags as drivers discover the circuit’s limits.

That makes qualifying execution particularly important. On an unfamiliar track, teams cannot assume every planned run will remain available.

An early representative banker lap may therefore carry greater value than usual, especially in Q1 and Q2.

The concern has a significant pre-event data point: Formula 3 testing at Madring reportedly produced 19 red flags and three damaged chassis. Formula 1 should not be expected to reproduce that outcome directly, but the test illustrates how quickly an unfamiliar circuit can interrupt planned running.

Tyre understanding starts with C2, C3 and C4

Pirelli has confirmed the C2, C3 and C4 compounds for Madrid, with the C2 designated hard, the C3 medium and the C4 soft.

Each driver receives two hard sets, three mediums and eight softs for the weekend. That gives teams a conventional three-step compound range, but the absence of previous Formula 1 race data means the real operating window remains unknown.

Madring’s fresh asphalt is smooth and expected to offer moderate grip. The first Friday runs will therefore establish how quickly the surface evolves, how easily each compound reaches temperature and whether sustained loading through La Monumental creates more degradation than simulations predict.

Long-run FP2 data should be especially valuable. If the medium proves durable while retaining competitive pace, it could become the primary race tyre. If degradation remains low, teams may have greater strategic flexibility than expected.

Antonelli arrives with championship control

Antonelli arrives with 267 points, 66 ahead of George Russell on 201. Lewis Hamilton is third with 191, Lando Norris has 171, Charles Leclerc 155 and Max Verstappen 127.

That gives Antonelli flexibility his rivals do not have. At a completely new circuit, the championship leader does not need to force an early advantage. A clean, high-scoring weekend would preserve control.

Russell and the chasing group need something different: they need Madring's uncertainty to create a larger points swing than Monza did.

Leclerc cleared for Madrid after Monza crash

Ferrari arrives in Madrid with one major uncertainty removed.

Charles Leclerc is fit to compete in the Spanish Grand Prix following further checks after his heavy Italian Grand Prix crash. The Ferrari driver suffered an impact exceeding 50G at Monza after losing control at Parabolica, having initially reported a brief vision issue immediately after the accident.

Subsequent assessments have cleared Leclerc for the Madrid weekend.

Ferrari has also avoided a second complication from the accident. The SF-26’s power unit did not sustain damage severe enough to require a complete replacement, meaning Leclerc will not take an engine-related grid penalty for the Spanish Grand Prix. Ferrari will replace the transmission casing instead.

That leaves the team able to approach Madring with its normal driver pairing and without a crash-related grid handicap.

The focus now shifts back to performance. Ferrari scored eight points at Monza and arrives at a completely new circuit 122 points behind Mercedes in the Constructors’ Championship.

Red Bull arrives with its substitute lineup unchanged

Madrid will also begin without Red Bull’s regular second driver.

Isack Hadjar will miss his third consecutive Grand Prix as his recovery from a wrist injury continues. Red Bull has confirmed that Liam Lawson will remain alongside Max Verstappen for the Spanish Grand Prix, while Yuki Tsunoda continues alongside Arvid Lindblad at Racing Bulls.

Lawson has now completed two races as Hadjar’s substitute, finishing seventh at Zandvoort before taking 14th at Monza. Tsunoda recorded 11th and 10th across the same two weekends after returning to the Racing Bulls cockpit.

The continuity should make Madrid slightly less disruptive than the initial Zandvoort substitution, but the new circuit raises the importance of Friday mileage. Lawson must simultaneously learn Madring and continue adapting to the Red Bull, while Tsunoda receives another opportunity to build on his points finish at Monza.

Hadjar will be present with Red Bull in Madrid but is not yet ready to return to the cockpit.

For Paddock Command, Lawson’s comparison with Verstappen will be one of the more useful secondary indicators during Friday practice. On a circuit where every driver starts without Formula 1 race experience, the size and character of that performance gap should provide another data point on Lawson’s adaptation to the RB22.

Mercedes has the strongest margin

Mercedes also enters Madrid with a substantial Constructors' Championship advantage: 468 points compared with Ferrari's 346, McLaren's 287 and Red Bull's 204.

That matters because an unfamiliar circuit rewards teams capable of gathering useful data quickly across both cars. Mercedes arrives with the strongest championship position, while Ferrari, McLaren and Red Bull each have an opportunity to exploit the new venue before established performance patterns develop.

Paddock Command FP1 watch listCar stability through La Monumental; braking confidence into the major stopping zones; tyre temperature and degradation over longer runs; ride quality across the hybrid circuit surface; and how quickly each team converges on a workable aerodynamic balance.

The first practice session will be unusually important

At most circuits, FP1 confirms or refines assumptions. At Madring, FP1 will create the first serious Formula 1 reference point.

The headline lap time will matter less than the rate of learning. A team that begins close to the correct operating window can spend Friday refining performance. A team that begins outside it may spend valuable practice time solving basic balance problems.

What Madrid can tell us

New circuits can temporarily disrupt the normal competitive order because experience has less value and adaptation has more.

Antonelli has just demonstrated that he can recover from an extreme grid disadvantage. Russell remains the closest championship challenger. Norris retains strong recent form. Ferrari and Red Bull still need weekends that change the trajectory rather than simply produce isolated results.

Madrid gives all of them a clean technical question: who understands the new circuit fastest?
