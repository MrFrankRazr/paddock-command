Madring Circuit Guide: What F1 Learned — and What Changes for 2027

Formula 1’s first weekend at Madring established the circuit’s technical character—and exposed an overtaking problem serious enough that Spanish Grand Prix organisers are already preparing changes for 2027.

Paddock Command editorial · Updated Sep 14, 2026
First Formula 1 race completed · 2027 layout review underway
LENGTH5.414 km
CORNERS22
RACE57 laps
2027Layout review underway

Madring finally has a Formula 1 baseline

The first Madrid weekend supplied something simulation could not: representative Formula 1 data.

Lap times improved by more than two seconds between FP1 and Lando Norris’s pole-position effort, underlining how aggressively the fresh circuit evolved as rubber accumulated.

That rapid evolution made Friday setup work unusually difficult, but teams will return with considerably better knowledge of the surface, braking references, tyre behaviour and optimum racing lines.

Madring is still young. It is no longer unknown.

Tyre management became one of the defining demands

The weekend also confirmed that tyre management matters heavily around Madring.

Drivers repeatedly found that extracting one-lap performance and maintaining tyre condition over a stint were different challenges. That made qualifying execution important without eliminating the need for careful race management.

The circuit therefore rewards more than outright peak grip. Drivers need enough tyre remaining late in a stint to attack, defend and remain stable through its faster loaded sections.

La Monumental remains Madring’s signature challenge

La Monumental delivered exactly what the circuit design promised: a technically unusual, visually distinctive banked corner unlike most conventional Formula 1 sequences.

Its sustained banking combines lateral load, vertical load and prolonged tyre stress. It should remain one of the primary engineering reference points whenever Formula 1 returns to Madrid.

But the inaugural weekend showed that Madring’s greatest problem lies elsewhere.

The first Grand Prix exposed the overtaking problem

Qualifying produced a demanding test. Racing produced much less movement.

The combination of medium- and high-speed corners, narrow approaches and braking zones that often begin while the car is still turning made passing exceptionally difficult.

That meant track position carried enormous value and strategic events had a disproportionate impact on the final order.

The lack of conventional overtaking also reduced the possibility of drivers recovering after losing position through strategy.

Turn 5 did not work as intended

The Turn 5 chicane following Madring’s longest straight was expected to become one of the circuit’s main passing zones.

Instead, the geometry made attacks difficult.

Driver feedback has focused on the number of corners where braking and steering overlap. When the leading car can cover the braking zone while already positioning for the next direction change, the following driver has fewer viable attacking lines.

That problem is now part of the official circuit review.

Organisers are preparing changes for 2027

The most important post-race development is that the discussion is no longer limited to driver criticism.

Spanish Grand Prix managing director Luis Garcia Abad says organisers have already received recommendations from the FIA and intend to work on improvements ahead of the 2027 event.

Madring has an advantage many street circuits do not: substantial portions of the venue are purpose-built and can be reconfigured when the temporary elements are installed again next year.

That gives Formula 1, the FIA and the promoter meaningful room to improve racing rather than simply adjusting barriers or DRS positioning.

The exact 2027 configuration has not yet been approved, so individual corner changes should still be treated as proposals rather than final plans.

Norris has offered one possible direction

Lando Norris has been among the most direct critics of the layout.

His suggested approach would simplify the sequence near Turns 18 and 19 by creating a more direct run into a wider, slower braking zone.

The theory is straightforward: lengthen the approach, reduce the complexity before the braking point and create a corner where multiple lines are possible.

Norris also identified the Turn 5-6-7 sequence as an area requiring deeper consideration.

Whether Formula 1 follows his exact proposals is less important than the underlying principle: Madring needs braking zones where following cars can remain close enough to attack.

The long pit lane created another limitation

Circuit racing quality is not only determined by overtaking.

Drivers also highlighted Madring’s long pit lane, which increased the time cost of stopping and discouraged teams from experimenting aggressively with alternative strategies.

A circuit with difficult overtaking can still produce tactical variation when undercuts, offsets and additional stops remain viable.

Madrid made those alternatives expensive.

That gives the organisers another variable to examine alongside the physical corner layout.

The VSC magnified the circuit problem

The Spanish Grand Prix also exposed a wider Formula 1 sporting issue.

Norris was leading when Lance Stroll’s stopped Aston Martin triggered a Virtual Safety Car. Because Norris had already passed pit entry, cars behind him could take advantage of the reduced VSC pit-loss while he could not.

A VSC stop in Madrid saved approximately 11 seconds compared with a normal green-flag stop.

Kimi Antonelli and Max Verstappen benefited from that strategic window, while Norris ultimately finished third.

The VSC did not make Antonelli’s victory invalid. It demonstrated how strongly neutralisation timing can affect a race when overtaking is difficult enough that lost track position cannot realistically be recovered.

VSC procedure is now part of the conversation

Norris has also called for Formula 1 to reconsider the way pit stops operate during a VSC.

Ideas discussed include temporarily closing pit entry except for emergencies or creating a system that gives competitors a more equivalent opportunity to stop.

Those concepts carry complications involving weather, damage and safety, and no regulation change has been approved.

For now, the existing procedure remains in place.

But after Madrid, both the circuit and the sporting system that shaped its first Grand Prix are under examination.

What Madring’s first race confirmedRapid track evolution; significant tyre-management demands; La Monumental as a genuine technical differentiator; exceptional value for qualifying and track position; difficult overtaking in the current configuration; and an outsized strategic effect from neutralisation timing.

What teams will know next time

When Formula 1 returns to Madrid, teams will arrive with historical data they lacked in 2026.

They will understand approximate grip development, tyre behaviour, setup direction, La Monumental’s loading characteristics and the strategic penalty associated with losing track position.

What they may not encounter is exactly the same circuit.

That changes the second-year engineering challenge. Teams will combine lessons from the inaugural Grand Prix with whatever modifications Formula 1, the FIA and the Spanish GP organisers eventually approve.

Madring’s first verdict

Madring succeeded as a technical and qualifying challenge.

Its first Grand Prix also demonstrated that a circuit can be difficult to drive without necessarily being easy to race on.

The most encouraging post-race development is that the problem has been acknowledged quickly.

Madrid now has a year of Formula 1 evidence, direct driver feedback and FIA recommendations to work from.

The next test for Madring is no longer whether Formula 1 can race there.

It is whether the circuit can use what it learned from 2026 to produce a better race in 2027.
